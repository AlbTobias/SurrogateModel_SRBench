In recent years, machine-learning methods have profoundly impacted research in the interdisciplinary fields of physics. However, most machine-learning models lack interpretability, and physicists doubt the credibility of their conclusions because they
cannot be combined with prior physical knowledge. Therefore, this review focuses on symbolic regression, which is an
interpretable machine-learning method. First, the relevant concepts of machine learning are introduced in conjunction with
induction. Next, we provide an overview of symbolic regression methods. Subsequently, the recent directions for the application
of symbolic regression methods in different subfields of physics are outlined, and an overview of the ways in which the
applications of symbolic regression have evolved in the realm of physics is provided. The major aim of this review is to introduce
the basic principles of symbolic regression and explain its applications in the field of physics.

1 Introduction
Machine learning is a branch of artificial intelligence (AI)
and computer science capable of providing solutions to
problems such as large-dataset analysis and identifying
paradigms within quantities of data beyond human-processing capabilities. In recent years, machine learning methods
have been used across various research fields and have made
significant contributions to physics research. The machinelearning process includes data collection, algorithm selection, feature engineering, model training, and results analysis.
Induction is essential to build a ration-based perception of
the world and plays a crucial role in physical research. For
example, in the history of physics research, the discovery of
the law of gravity marked the origin of classical physics and
Planck’s fit to blackbody radiation was the doorway into
quantum mechanics; both of these were applications of induction. Taking the discovery of the law of gravitation, the
induction process can be summarized as follows: observing
phenomena (Brahe) → recording data (Brahe) → processing
data (Kepler) → inductive analysis (Kepler). A comparison
shows that the induction and machine learning processes
have some similarities. By thinking of data as a description
of phenomena and models as a representation of conclusions,
we can say that induction (or machine learning) is the pro-

Machine learning covers several types of algorithms.
Different machine learning algorithms have different principles and operation modes that perform well on different
problems and return models of different forms. Unfortunately, most major machine learning methods, such as
random forests (RF), support vector machines (SVM), and
powerful artificial neural networks (ANN), are all black-box
models.
Black-box models pay the price of interpretability to ensure model performance because of their complexity. The
lack of interpretability raises questions about whether the
model is limited in generalizations. Therefore, an abundance
of high-quality training data is frequently necessitated to
enhance the performance of the model to alleviate overfitting
and improve the scope of the application. We believe that a
balance exists among the amount of data required for model
training, complexity, and interpretability. There is no best
machine learning model for all situations, and choosing the
most suitable model according to the unique problem is the
key to using machine learning methods for research.
Physicists often prioritize interpretable physical logic over
obtaining a model that can only provide an answer to a single
problem. Therefore, we recommend that physicists use
symbolic regression (SR) methods, which are interpretable
machine-learning methods that are suitable for small datasets. Sect. 2 of this review provides a basic overview of
symbolic regression methods, emphasizing the similarities
between SR methods and the data-processing induction step,
both of which provide a result that is sufficiently simple for
human analysis. In some studies, researchers have used their
own SR method to re-implement Newton’s workflow. For
example, Austel et al. [1] rediscovered Kepler’s law and
Lemos et al. [2] found an analytical expression equivalent to
Newton’s law of gravitation.
2 Symbolic regression
2.1 Algorithm overview
The symbolic regression (SR) [3] algorithm is a machine
learning technique that can search the space of mathematical
expressions based on a given dataset. During the search, SR
identifies optimal mathematical expressions by minimizing
the error metrics. Unlike most machine learning algorithms,
SR can train a model using even very small training datasets
[4], and it outperforms other models for small datasets [5]. In
contrast to regression methods built on the known form (such
as linear regression [6,7] and kernel-based regression [8])
that can only find the formulas corresponding to the basic
form to solve a specific kind of problem, SR searches for the
parameters and form of equations simultaneously [9], which
provides a more varied searching space and can solve general
problems without a priori assumptions [9,10]. This property
allows SR to provide interpretable results for physical problems without knowing the equation forms. The free generation of formulas without prior assumptions exponentially
increases the complexity of the model, making SR an NPhard (nondeterministic polynomial-time hard) [11] problem
in principle [12]. Considering the limitations of computing
power, large instances of such problems cannot be solved
using accurate algorithms. In general, researchers adopt effective approximation algorithms to solve these problems
and replace the brute-force search with other strategies.
The concept of SR can be traced back to the BACON
program [13]. Since then, several strategies have been applied in SR, genetic programming (GP) [14-20], deep
learning [21,22], fast function extraction [23], improved
programming [24], Bayesian methods [25], MINLP methods
[26-29], compressed sensing methods [30-32], etc. [33-38].
Recently, La Cava et al. [39] established a reliable standard
to measure the performance of different strategies applied in
SR methods.
In the field of physics, GPSR is currently the most widely
used SR method with the widest range of applications. In
addition to GPSR, the following strategies to achieve SR are
also worthy of attention. Symbolic regression based on
compressive sensing strategy (CSSR) methods [30,31,40,41]
can handle larger feature spaces without additional processing of highly correlated features. This feature makes CSSR
methods perform well in mining descriptors of material
properties and have been widely applied [32,42-52]. Equation learner (EQL) is based on fully differentiable neural
networks, which allow end-to-end training using backpropagation, and implement symbolic regression [53]. Many
symbolic regression algorithms have been developed based
on EQL in recent years [54-57]. Cozad and Sahinidis [26]
proposed the strictly deterministic mixed-integer nonlinear
programming (MINLP) [58] formulation for solving the SR
problem to global optimality. Optimal solutions have important implications for some physical problems. As a result,
MINLP-SR has gained many developments and applications
[27,29,59]. Another algorithm worth paying attention to is
AI-Feynman [60], which is a specialized SR algorithm for

2.2 Genetic programming-based symbolic regression
Inspired by the natural law of “survival of the fittest” and
genetics represented by Mendel’s laws, genetic algorithms
(GAs) [61,62] were designed. By using genetic algorithms,
computational requirements can be significantly reduced
compared with exhaustive methods. Koza [14] developed
GP, a method that converts mathematical expressions into
formula trees. The GP algorithm has been widely used in the
computer field, and GPSR is an application of GP in the
direction of SR. GPSR relies on randomly generated and
“mutated” function expressions to find higher-scoring
function expressions. This method reduces computing costs
and can obtain satisfactory results in most cases (though it
does not guarantee that the final solution will be optimal).
Recently, researchers created a widely used machinelearning library called scikit-learn [63] using Python. During
system compilation, developers added GPSR to scikit-learn
as an extension in the form of a gplearn module (https://
gplearn.readthedocs.io/en/stable/index.html). To date,
gplearn has become a popular and mature implementation
scheme of the SR algorithm in Python, and this method has
been widely applied in the fields of physics [64-66]. Various
GPSR algorithms have different architectures and implementations. This review will introduce the basic principles of GPSR, using gplearn as an example.
2.2.1 Randomly generating formulas to build a search
space
First, researchers define the function set, the terminal set, the
algorithm control parameter, the fitness function, and the
termination criterion of SR according to the needs of the
actual problem. The function set includes mathematical operators, mathematical functions, and conditional operators.
The terminal set includes the independent variables and
constants that build the equations. The algorithm control
parameters include the probability of occurrence of different
genetic operations and population size. A fitness function is
used to assess the appropriateness of the model. The termination criterion was set as the aborted condition of the loop.
Next, the elements in the function and terminal sets are
combined to generate unselected random formulas that can
be represented by trees. In these formula trees, the elements
in the function are set as internal nodes, and the elements in
the terminal function are set as terminal nodes. Each tree
corresponds to an independent solution to the problem.
2.2.2 Narrowing the problem space to find a better solution by filtering formulas
After the initial formula is generated, GPSR enters the
generation loop. In this step, the genetic SR method evaluates all formulas to select the “fitter” formulas from the
current generation formulas. In general, MSE, RMSE, MAE,
and other common error metrics used in machine learning
can be effectively implemented in general SR problems.
Most GPSR methods control the formula complexity by
adding a penalty term to the fitness evaluation criterion to
avoid overfitting. By adding the penalty term, the formulas
that have higher complexity have lower evaluation scores;
therefore, they are more likely to be eliminated in the elimination step. Gplearn has a built-in covariant thrift method
that considers both the original fitness and complexity of the
formula and can dynamically adjust the size of the complexity penalty automatically.
After distinguishing the “fitters” and outputting them to
the pool, the algorithm enters the reproduction loop, which
performs genetic operations on the “fitters” to generate the
next generation. In this study, we use the genetic operations
contained in gplearn as an example. A schematic of the genetic operation is shown in Figure 2.
Not limited to the gplearn method, these genetic operations
are commonly used in other types of GP. Koza [14] provides
a more in-depth understanding of these concepts and their
origins. The GPSR loops through the reproduction loop until
it reaches the termination criterion set, returns to the beginning of the generation loop, and the generation process is
repeated until a generation reaches a preset termination
condition. A flowchart of GPSR is shown in Figure 3.
2.3 Related work
Symbolic regression methods have gained increasing attention in recent years. Except for our review, there are numerous other reviews available for readers to reference.
Wang et al. [67] mainly explored the application of SR
methods in materials science. Angelis et al. [68] provided a
comprehensive survey of various SR methods and their applications in diverse domains, such as materials, finance,
medicine, engineering, and some non-physical disciplines.
Makke and Chawla [69] presented a comprehensive and
structured review of SR methods, discussing the advantages
and limitations of different SR methods. Liu and Senftle [45]
focus on the application of SR (GPSR and CSSR) in the
catalysis literature and provide related examples. Distinct
from other reviews, our review focused specifically on the

3 Application of symbolic regression in the field
of physics
As a supervised model, SR can discover hidden mathematical formulas from data to predict target variables. By using
SR, potential formulas can be searched from data for physicists to analyze, thereby reducing human workload. Furthermore, SR methods can provide a new cognitive path
away from human inertia, leading to conclusions that are
easily overlooked or difficult to derive from the human brain.
The purpose of this review is to demonstrate the application
of SR in physics fields and provide readers with a basic
understanding of its application methods and potential future
development directions. In each subsection of sect. 3, we
present practical examples to demonstrate the application of
SR.
3.1 Symbolic regression applied in classical mechanics
Askari and Crevecoeur [70] developed an evolutionary
symbolic sparse regression module suitable for multibody
system dynamics. They tested their method on a two-DoF
spring-mass system, a crank-slider mechanism, and a sliding
mass subjected to friction. Liu and Tegmark [71] presented
AI Poincaré, a machine learning method that can find conserved quantities from gravitational three-body and other
dynamical system trajectory data. They apply their method to
five Hamiltonian systems and it discover all exactly conserved quantities. Udrescu and Tegmark [72] built a workflow capable of discovering equations of motion from
synthetic video. They used Auto-Encoder as “symbolic
progression” to learn trajectories from “video frame” and
used SR to discover formulas based on selected features.
In this section, we choose the study of Schmidt and Lipson
[9] as a representative example and provide a detailed introduction. This study is considered representative, and the
genetic algorithm, Eureqa, designed in this study is regarded
as the gold standard for SR and has been widely applied.
Schmidt and Lipson [9] used a motion tracking system to
record the positions of objects in a single pendulum, double
pendulum, and simple harmonic vibration systems over time
as datasets and then used partial derivative pairs to create a
criterion that can examine the ability of a formula to reflect
the intrinsic correlations of a system. Their SR method not
only identifies physically significant constant laws but also
identifies physically significant equations that are nontrivial
conservation laws. They used their method to rediscover the
expressions for Hamilton’s equation, Lagrange’s equation,
and other geometric and momentum conservation law forms
without prior knowledge in the fields of physics, kinematics,
or geometry. Depending on the physical quantities provided,
the algorithm determines interpretable laws of mechanics or
geometry. For instance, in the study of a single pendulum

3.2 Symbolic regression applied in astrophysics
Matchev et al. [73] used SR on synthetic data for the transit
radii of hot Jupiter exoplanets to derive an analytical formula. Wadekar et al. [74] used SR to search for augmentations of the widely used YSZ-M scaling relation. They found
that a new mass proxy combined Y200c and gas concentration. In their other work, they modeled the halo environment and used PySR to find expressions that included
the effect of the halo environment on its Hi mass [75].
Barsotti et al. [76] used AutoML techniques and the RBEIM
framework to predict gravitational waveform surrogates
from compact binaries and found that Eureqa (GPSR) demonstrated superior performance among 87 methods. Wong
and Cranmer [77] demonstrated a strategy to automatically
discover interpretable gravitational wave population models
and find a new model as proof. Cranmer et al. [78] combined
graph neural networks and SR to develop a formula for
predicting dark matter density.
We chose Lemos et al.’s study [2] to demonstrate the application of the SR in astrophysics. This study not only demonstrates the ability of SR to obtain comprehensible
symbolic formulas by mining astronomical data but also illustrates the application directions of SR in combining
physical prior knowledge and integrating it with other machine learning algorithms. This study harmoniously combined prior physical knowledge, SR, and neural networks,
demonstrating the potential of neural networks to simplify
complex problems and the influential role of prior physical
knowledge in guiding machine learning.

3.3 Symbolic regression applied in fluid mechanics
Computing the exact solutions for complex fluid dynamics is
difficult. Consequently, researchers often resort to simpler
approximate functions as substitutes for the true function,
thereby reducing the problem’s complexity. Manually finding a reliable approximate function is time-consuming;
however, as a machine learning method that returns functions, SR has great potential for finding approximate functions. El Hasadi and Padding [82] used semi-supervised
GPSR to study the fluid drag experienced by ellipsoidal and
spherocylindrical particles with arbitrary aspect ratios and
provided new drag correlations. Ansari et al. [83] combined
active learning and SISSO to create a framework to obtain
symbolic equations between system variables from computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations. They proved the
effectiveness of their method by recovering an empirical
equation for the pressure drop in a bent pipe and finding a
new equation for predicting the backflow in a heart valve
under aortic insufficiency. Miloševic et al. [84] overview
existing models that unified the laminar and turbulent formulations of flow friction and used the Eureqa mine with two
new formulations. Sofos et al. [85] used GPSR to present a
Lennard-Jones fluid descriptor based on density and temperature values.
In this section, we present two examples in detail. In the
first example, Praks et al. [86] utilized SR to find the explicit
approximations of the Colebrook equation. In the second
example, Reinbold et al. [87] utilized SR to summarize a
model exhibiting a thin-layer fluid with complex spatiotemporal behavior.
The Colebrook equation [88] is widely used for calculating
the flow friction factor λ of turbulent flow pipeline transportation problems. However, λ is implicitly given by the
Colebrook equation. Therefore, it is worthwhile to find a
formula in the form of λ ≈ f(Re, ε/D) to process realistic
problems. Brkić [89] introduced the existing explicit approximation of the implicit Colebrook equation in their review. The primary objective of this study was to find
approximate equations with explicit structures and low
computational costs. The SR method, which constrains the
model complexity and returns function expressions, is highly
adaptive to this research goal.
Praks and Brkić [86] generated 90000 evenly uniformly
distributed {Re, ε/D} and calculated their corresponding
flow friction factors λ with the Colebrook equation. These
data pairs cover turbulent flow in pipes from practically
smooth to very rough for Reynolds numbers ranging from
4000 to 108
. Initially, they employed Eureqa to fit a function
that, while not particularly precise, serves as a framework.
Subsequently, the fixed-point iteration procedure was used
on the imprecise function, and the accuracy continually improved as the number of iterations increased. Finally, the
Padé approximation can be used as an inexpensive and accurate approximation of a logarithm.
The best approximation function found by the SR can
explicitly represent the flow friction coefficient with only
two logarithmic forms, and the relative error between λ

approximate equations with varying degrees of precision and
complexity for researchers to choose from. In the second

physical models based on incomplete experimental data by
introducing domain knowledge from physics. This example
shows that SR, as a white-box model, can be closely integrated with prior knowledge and, therefore, has the unique
advantage that black-box machine learning models do not
possess.
3.4 Symbolic regression applied in molecular dynamics
The interatomic potential is fundamental to molecular dynamics research, directly related to the success of calculations and the reliability of the conclusions. The calculation of
the potential energy surface with first-principles methods can
be computationally expensive. To solve this problem, researchers have used machine learning techniques to enhance
the efficacy [90].
In the molecular dynamics field, symbolic regression is
typically implemented using GP and its variants. Makarov
and Metiu [91] created a GPSR method named directed genetic search and used it to rewrite the Morse potential as an
example, demonstrating that the method can fit the potential
of a diatomic or a triatomic molecule. Brown et al. [92] used
a new parallel GP approach that employed large-scale
computations to rediscover the exact potential functional
forms and parameter values. In their study, the Hierarchical
fair competition (HFC) strategy performed better than the
standard generational evolutionary strategy. Bellucci and
Coker [93] used a parallel multilevel GP to obtain an accurate empirical valence bond model for proton transfer in 3-
hydroxy-gamma-pyrone in the gas phase and protic solvents.
Hernandez et al. [94] discovered a multibody potential model
for copper by using GPSR and comprehensively evaluated
their model. Their SR potential function has a speed similar
to that of the EAM-type models and requires less training
data than neural network potentials. In their recent study,
they test the transferability of their Cu interatomic potential
models in other face-centered cubic transition metals. To the
elements close to Cu on the periodic table, their model performs well in balance of accuracy and complexity [95]. For
various states of Lennard-Jones (LJ) fluids, Papastamatiou et
al. [96] used SR to discover analytical expressions that describe the self-diffusion coefficient as a function of other
physical properties. In this study, the application of methods
in data subcategories can provide better equations for the
description of the data.
This review provides a detailed introduction to the study of
Slepoy et al. [97] to briefly demonstrate the basic application
of SR in the field of molecular dynamics and showcase the
ability of GPSR to mine formulas from datasets. Burlacu et
al. [98] discussed the application of SR models of interatomic potentials and provided a method that can model
atomic potentials from snapshots of atomic positions and
associated potential energy. Slepoy et al. [97] combined
GPSR and parallel tempering (PT) [99] to build the SR
method. PT combines local and global sampling, and its introduction can prevent GPSR from falling into local optima.
They examined whether their methods could discover compact force-field functions that accurately represent the potential energy surface of condensed systems. Therefore, the
Lennard-Jones potential function, a simple, classic, and
widely used function in the field of molecular modeling and
condensed matter systems, was chosen for testing. The
Lennard-Jones potential is a two-body potential used to simulate the potential energy of interactions between two nonbonding atoms or molecules (such as rare gas elements).
Details of the Lennard-Jones potential is shown in Figure 8.
The training set consists of 10 configurations of 10 particles placed in a three-dimensional domain in the field of 3σ*

condition and effective pair distances in the range. By generating atomic configurations and calculating the energy of
each configuration using the Lennard-Jones potential, four

We consider that SR holds the potential to positively impact the fitting of potential energy surfaces by discovering
symbolic expression formulas. These formulas may be

the fitted potential energy surfaces.
4 Conclusions
In this review, we delve into the parallels between machine
learning and induction pipelines, elucidating how symbolic
regression methods serve as useful tools in physics research.
To provide a fundamental understanding of symbolic regression, this review introduces the principles and characteristics of genetic programming-based symbolic
regression. Sect. 3 of the review navigates through the
practical applications of SR in various physics subdivisions.
Based on existing research, we examine the combination of
SR methods with physics and deliberate on potential avenues
for future research.
SR provides advantages for small datasets, offering potentially interpretable results in the form of symbolic expressions. Prior to applying SR, researchers need to ensure
the alignment between the characteristics of the problem and
the fundamental attributes of the SR method. Given the diverse implementation methods of SR, it becomes imperative
to understand their distinct characteristics.
The combinations of SR methods and physical knowledge
can be multifaceted. Researchers have the option to employ
existing SR methods for model training and leverage prior
knowledge in various stages of machine learning as a guide.
Another approach involves crafting specific SR methods that
integrate prior physical knowledge. The incorporation of
prior knowledge often yields positive outcomes, as elucidated in sect. 3. Not only can it impose constraints to drive
conclusions with enhanced physical meaning, but it can also
serve to augment training data and mitigate the impact of
data noise and missing data.
This review covers the subfields of classical physics that
which analytical formulas are dominant. SR is also noted to
be applied in other subfields, such as nuclear physics [100],
and condensed matter physics [65]. Since SR can be considered as an interpretable machine learning approach, it
naturally can be applied to black-box problems. For example, the exchange-correlation function is a long-term
puzzle in the field of density functional theory. Recently, Ma
et al. [101] created a symbolic functional evolutionary search
(SyFES), providing interpretable accurate exchange-correlation functionals in the field of quantum physics. We anticipate that the development of new SR methods based on
the needs of the specific topics would definitely expand the
application scope of SR in other physics sub-fields.
In summary, symbolic regression is a machine-learning
method that has many similarities with induction. It can mine
symbolic formulas from extensive data. Depending on the
requirement, researchers have the option to either try to
provide a physics-based interpretation of the obtained formulas or simply use them as a mathematically convenient
alternative to solve computationally expensive problems. We
consider that SR holds vast potential for further development
and application within the field of physics. However, in
scientific discovery, scientists usually encounter high-dimensional inputs (e.g., images, observations) for which they
do not know what collective/macroscopic variables (e.g.,
entropy, energy, quasi-particles) are important. Thus, combining SR with the discovery of these important variables
should be a very important direction.
